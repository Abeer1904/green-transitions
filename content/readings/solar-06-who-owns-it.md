The same panels can be owned and paid for in very different ways. The directory describes six models.

## 1. Household rooftop: pay once, save every month
The household buys a small rooftop system with a subsidy (PM Surya Ghar). The return comes from **avoided electricity bills**, plus export credit where net metering works. It is the **simplest** model: nothing shared, no group to hold together.

## 2. Solar irrigation pump: farmer pays a small share
The farmer pays a small share; the rest comes as subsidy (PM KUSUM). Diesel is replaced by sun. With a **universal controller**, the same pump can also run a **chaff cutter and a flour mill**, so the pump is used for more than one job. Easiest to scale.

## 3. Community or cooperative pump
A cooperative, FPO or water user group owns a shared pump. A trained local operator collects a **per-hour or per-acre water fee** and a power tariff. It spreads the cost and creates a **paid local job.**

> **The warning: Dharnai, Bihar.** A 100 kW micro-grid served 400+ households, pumps, a school and a health centre. Within weeks, the state grid arrived, and it was cheaper. Households on the micro-grid fell from **380 to 120**. The **batteries died in year 3** and the system has not been maintained since.

## 4. Grid-injection cooperative
Farmers keep the pump for their own use but **sell surplus power** to the DISCOM under a long-term agreement (PPA), so the pump also earns income.

> **The success: Dhundi, Gujarat.** Six farmers pooled into a solar pump cooperative with a **25-year PPA** with MGVCL. They earned **₹5.26 lakh in one year** by selling surplus power.

## 5. RESCO / OPEX model for institutions
A developer **owns and maintains** the rooftop system on the school, Panchayat Bhawan or health centre. The institution pays a **lower monthly bill than the grid rate**, with **no upfront cost** and **no maintenance burden** for the panchayat.

## 6. Government-anchored full-village solarisation
The state or Centre funds a village-scale ground-mounted plant, plus a battery, plus subsidised rooftops, run through a state agency.

## And under every model: the technician service economy
No matter who owns the panels, a trained local technician pool can earn from **installation labour, annual maintenance contracts (AMC), cleaning rounds and repairs.**

:::note
What Dharnai teaches
Dharnai was not a failure of panels. It was a failure of **cost comparison, maintenance and ownership**. Before building anything, ask: what will electricity cost from the grid when it arrives? Who pays to replace the battery in year 3? Who is trained to do it?
:::

:::
