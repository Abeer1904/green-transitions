## What they looked at

This paper asks what a green skill is and who gets to build one. The authors read close to 300 papers and reports and interviewed **24 people** from climate, education and gender organisations. The paper covers skills in general. Solar training is one case.

## What they found

- **Green skills are treated as technical skills.** Countries' climate plans lean toward skills like renewable energy technology and water management. Only **24%** of the plans mention education for children and youth.
- **The technical path leaves women out.** Girls are **35%** of students in STEM-related fields worldwide, though their results are as good as boys' or better in most countries.
- **Skills are not the main barrier for women.** In a survey the paper cites, women in renewable energy ranked lack of STEM skills as the lowest barrier. Social and cultural norms came first. Next: too few gender-sensitive policies, then too little gender-sensitive training.
- **Three kinds of green skill.** Specific skills for green jobs, such as engineering. Life skills, such as critical thinking. Transformative skills, such as reading how power works and acting together.
- **Skills need openings.** The paper says skills must come with opportunity structures: policies, institutions, networks and norms. Discriminatory lending is its example of a block.
- **Green work is wider than energy.** Teachers and community health workers do work that is already low carbon, and mostly done by women.

## What it means for a village like Malai

A solar course opens a door only if people can walk through it. Ask who hears about it, who can travel to it, who can get a loan afterwards and who the family lets take the work.

In this paper's terms, running a shared pump and speaking up in a group count as green skills. So does the work of village teachers and health workers.

:::facilitator
For facilitators
- **Method and sample:** Conceptual review with a feminist lens. Nearly 300 grey and academic sources, picked for relevance and coded. 24 interviews with staff of multilateral, government and non-governmental organisations, chosen to cover countries of different income levels. No new data were collected.
- **Where and when:** Global scope. Published 2022. Funded by the Malala Fund.
- **How strong the evidence is:** Strong as a map of ideas and definitions. It does not test whether any training programme works. Examples such as CAMFED in sub-Saharan Africa are described, not evaluated.
- **Limits and what does not transfer to Haryana:** The paper does not discuss India or Haryana. It focuses on adolescent girls and national education policy, with no findings on solar or village women.
:::
